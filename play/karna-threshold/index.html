The Karna Threshold

Why does a decent person keep carrying a failing consignment, and feel noble doing it? Three players hold one cargo: the Seller (Bailor), Bailee n+1 (you), and the Buyer (Holder). You will feel the pull of identity, gratitude and shame, then open the lab and test whether the pull slides, tips, or remembers.

1. Sliding or tipping?

Set a decision-maker with the sliders. The curves show the chance they keep the failing course as the rational advantage of switching grows. A gentle curve is drift (sliding). A cliff is a tipping point.

Additive only (no interactions, soft k)With identity × shame interactionsInteractions and a sharp k

2. Does the decision remember?

Raise the advantage of switching from 0 to 1.2, then lower it back. With a prior-commitment term (η), the way down is not the way up. The gap between the two thresholds is the hysteresis.

Rising advantage (committed start)Falling advantage (after switching)

3. The third offer

Same people, same cargo, two Krishnas. One offers only the kingdom (just switch). The other offers a way to protect the Bailor's dignity, honour the debt and still fix the chain. The hypothesis, written into the model as a relief on the identity × shame terms, is that this lets people leave without feeling they have abandoned themselves. This run tests the model's own assumption, so treat it as a thought experiment, not evidence.

4. Why R² near 1 is a warning

We generate 4,000 synthetic decisions from a hidden dynamic-threshold process, fit three models on 70%, and score them on the unseen 30%. Then add a feature that looks like a predictor but is really the outcome in disguise (a loyalty statement made after the decision) and watch the fit become perfect and worthless.

Guilt and shame pull in opposite directions, so they are separate variables. Merging them would erase the very effect we are looking for.

The equations used here

B = β0 + β1·I + β2·G + β3·S + β4·P + β5·C + β6·J − β7·M + γ1·(I·S) + γ2·(G·S) + γ3·(I·P) + η·Y(t−1) P(stay) = 1 / (1 + e^( −k · (B − β8·R) )) D = U_best − U_chosen (welfare given up; computed from the money only, never from the psychological variables)

H1 Sliding: rising commitment cost raises resistance gradually. H2 Tipping: identity × shame makes the rise disproportionately sharp. H3 Hysteresis: after public commitment, reversing needs a bigger incentive than avoiding the commitment would have.

Profit and loss for the three players

One consignment, V = 100, seven legs. Every leg you keep the original course, the hidden hazard compounds and may damage the cargo. At the end, condition q sets everyone's result.

Seller (Bailor) = 30·q − 3·(times you switched openly) + 2·(honourable exits) Bailee n+1 = fees (3 per leg) − switching costs − liability, liability = 0.9·max(0, 80 − 100·q) Buyer (Holder) = 60·q − 30

The Bailee also has a felt score: loyalty pays a little pride each time you stay, and switching openly costs 10·S·I in shame. That is the "psychologically corrupted reward function": a perfectly rational optimiser can be steering toward a worse world because the felt score and the real welfare have come apart.

Honest limits

Nothing here is data. The coefficients are chosen to make the mechanisms visible. A near-perfect R² on synthetic data proves nothing about people. A real test needs experiments that manipulate identity threat, gratitude, publicness and the size of the benefit, and scores the model on decisions it has never seen.
